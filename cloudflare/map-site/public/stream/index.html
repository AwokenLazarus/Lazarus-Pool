Whitepaper · XBTLightning Stream: pay as it arrives

Anyone who runs a Lightning node should be able to sell a service to the network (a film, an AI model, hashrate, an API, software of their own) and be paid for it piece by piece as it is delivered, by people and by AI agents alike.

Lightning Stream does that on ordinary XBT Lightning. The buyer pays a small ticket ahead, the seller counts what it delivers to the millisatoshi and signs a receipt for every answer, and the buyer can lose no more than one ticket. For pieces too small to pay one by one, a fair roll decides which pieces are paid.

Draft 11 · 27 October 2026 · Lightning Stream (source). The app is released for Umbrel, StartOS and Linux and runs on XBT mainnet: Try it says how. It has been tested on mainnet between our own nodes, with small amounts, and is not audited; see Status.

Lightning made payments fast. Stream tickets make them continuous.

1The problem

More and more of what we buy arrives over time: the tokens of a model's answer, the minutes of a film, the work of a miner, the thousandth call to an API. Machines are now buyers as often as people, and the prices are tiny.

Most ways to pay for that ask someone to trust someone. Pay up front and you trust the seller to deliver; pay afterwards and the seller trusts you; a subscription trusts a company with your card. Lightning [3] already moves small payments between strangers in a moment, with nobody holding the money in the middle. What it lacks is a way to pay for something that arrives in thousands of pieces without making every piece wait for a payment of its own. Lightning Stream adds that part, and leaves the rest of Lightning as it is.

2The idea

Pay a small ticket before the service starts. Let the seller draw it down, exactly, as it delivers. Pay the next ticket when it runs out. A held ticket that is never used comes back, and credit left over is spent first the next time.

That is the whole addition. Lightning Stream is built on Lightning, not beside it: Lightning's own routing, liquidity and nodes carry everything, and Lightning Stream runs no network and no hub, and never holds anyone's money. Each person pays from, and is paid to, their own node. A seller needs an lnd-family node, in practice Lightning Fork [61], the fork of lnd [62] that runs on XBT. A Core Lightning node can buy and route, but cannot sell held tickets today.

3How a ticket works

A ticket is an ordinary Lightning payment of under 1,000 sat: about 900 at today's shops. It comes in two kinds.

A prepaid ticket is a plain payment the seller collects as it arrives. The seller then serves from that credit and counts every request against it in millisatoshis. This is what most calls on mainnet use today.

A held ticket turns an ordinary payment round. Normally the seller picks the secret that unlocks a payment, so the money is the seller's the moment it arrives. With a held ticket the buyer picks the secret, so the payment sits committed along the route and nobody can take it until the buyer releases the secret.

Terms
The seller answers the first request with its price and terms: its Lightning node, the size of a ticket and how long one lives.
Lock
For a held ticket the wallet sends only the hash of its secret, and the seller's node issues a hold invoice, which can be paid but not collected until the secret is shown. Hold invoices are Joost Jager's, in lnd since 2019 [5].
Pay
When the credit runs out, the wallet pays the next ticket, or releases the next secret. No request waits for a payment: the next ticket is ready in the background.
Stop
The seller cancels unused held tickets and the money is free at once. If it does not, they expire and the money returns by itself.

A payment the payer releases in a second step is Hiroki Gondo's idea, "stuckless payments" [6]; held tickets get that effect from hold invoices, which Lightning nodes already have. Its price is that the secret no longer proves payment. The proof is the seller's signed receipt, bound to each request, together with the payment on the buyer's own node.

What each side risks. The buyer can lose at most one ticket: a seller can take a ticket and stop serving, but it never has the next one. Unused held tickets are locked, not lost, for a few hours at most. The seller serves only from credit it has been paid or a ticket it has collected.

Why tickets stay small and few. A held ticket takes a payment slot on every channel it crosses, and a payment below a channel's dust limit cannot be claimed on the chain. So the wallet holds only a couple of tickets at a time, never below 546 sat, and at mainnet's fee rates it prepays rather than holding a ticket through a routing node. Kyle Santiago pointed out both limits, and the defaults follow from them. Slots and dust has the figures.

4Smaller than a ticket: the lottery

Some pieces are worth less than any payment worth sending: a tenth of a sat for one piece of a file shared by forty sellers. The answer is old, and it is David Wheeler's and Ronald Rivest's [33][34]: roll a fair die for each piece. Most rolls pay nothing; now and then one wins and pays an amount worth sending. The odds make the average the price. At a tenth of a sat a piece and a win of 64 sat, one piece in 640 wins.

Neither side can steer a roll: the seller fixes its half of every roll before the session, the buyer adds its half after each piece arrives, and both can work out the result. The price is exact on average and uneven in a short run, the way a miner's income is. In the form that is built, the buyer pays after a win with an ordinary Lightning payment, so nothing is locked and a buyer can pay many sellers at once. Its weakness is plain: a buyer can see a win and not pay, and a seller stops serving such a buyer after about one win. A form in which a win pays itself, first built by Gerhart, Taylor and Thyagarajan [44], is designed and will not be built before a cryptographer has reviewed it. The lottery in detail has both.

5The wallet: paying over HTTP, within rules you sign

A seller puts a gateway in front of a web service it already runs. The gateway answers a first request with 402 Payment Required and its terms, in the shape the x402 standard gives that status [22], and signs a receipt for every answer after that.

The buyer's side is a wallet that holds the keys and pays through the owner's own Lightning node. It was made first for the hardest case, an AI agent, which should be able to spend but never to hold a key, because a model can be tricked by what it reads. The agent asks the wallet to pay, over the Model Context Protocol [26], and the wallet decides. A person uses the same wallet through the app.

  • Spending rules the owner signs. The safe starter rules allow 2,000 sat a payment, 10,000 a day, 30,000 a week and 10,000 a seller, and ask the owner above 1,000. Only sellers the owner allowed are paid.
  • Real money only when asked for. On mainnet the wallet pays nothing until the owner confirms, in so many words, that it pays with real money.
  • Its own books. It counts what its own node paid, routing fees included, never a number a seller sends, and a cap is a hard cap.

6What it sells

Films and files
Priced per MiB and played as they arrive; a buyer pays for the bytes it receives, and a stream it stops costs no more than the limit it set, with a seller on the current release; an older seller can overdraw at most the credit already paid, one ticket.
An AI model
Priced per 1,000 tokens of its answer, counted token by token.
Hashrate
A hashmaker lists its miners and a hashtaker rents them into its own DATUM Gateway [66], paying for each slice of work after it is delivered: only shares the hashtaker's own gateway accepted are counted. (Names from Kilombino.)
Any web service
The gateway meters an answer per request, per byte, per token or per second, so nothing in it is specific to these three.

Buying and selling are one app. A shop's offer is a listing it signs and publishes on Nostr relays, so buyers can find it without a directory of ours.

7What it costs

A Lightning node on each side
A buyer needs a node with a channel; a seller needs an lnd-family node with room to receive, online while it serves.
Money tied up
Unused held tickets are locked until cancelled or expired, for hours if a seller vanishes. Unspent credit on a prepaid ticket stays with that seller until the buyer comes back.
Routing
Each ticket pays the usual routing fee. Held tickets take slots on every channel of their route, which is why they stay few, small and short-lived [10].
An uneven lottery
Paid by lottery, income and cost even out only over many wins, and the seller goes first by about one win per new buyer.
What others see
Nothing on the chain ties a buyer to a seller, but nodes along a route see a run of similar payments to one seller.

The costs, stated plainly has the figures.

8Prior art

Nothing in a ticket is a new mechanism: it is the hold invoice [5], used for what "stuckless payments" were proposed for [6], bound to HTTP 402 and to a spending policy the owner signs. Tickets sit between a prepaid balance, such as L402 [23], where the buyer can lose all of it, and a payment per piece, such as podcast streaming over keysend [8][9], where every piece waits for its payment. Cashu pays per request privately, but a mint holds the money [27]; nobody holds a ticket.

Paying by lottery goes back to Wheeler and Rivest [33][34], with later forms by Micali and Rivest [35] and by Pass and shelat [36], and lotteries run today by Orchid [39] and Livepeer [40]. For Bitcoin it was raised by Mike Caldwell [41], Tadge Dryja [42] and Oleksandr Kurbatov [43]. The lottery as built adds only the same binding to HTTP and a signed policy. Prior art on the design page has the full discussion.

9Status

Released. Lightning Stream is one app for buying and selling: in a community store for Umbrel, in our registry for StartOS, and as a Linux package. Try it has the setup, and the changelog every version.

On XBT mainnet, between our own nodes. Since 26 October 2026 a fresh install from the published packages has bought films and AI answers from our shop, a node box of ours has bought from a second shop of ours, and a rental of hashrate has been paid. Every charge matched the meter to the millisatoshi; Tests and numbers has the figures. On StartOS, the app's access to the node through Lightning Fork's grant was tested on mainnet; a paid buy on StartOS has not run yet. Nobody outside the project has used it yet. An invited test is next.

Reviewed, not audited. Chris Guida reviewed the design and the published code twice in October 2026: first the earlier design and its code, then the ticket design, the lottery and the tree (Progress and status). Kyle Santiago read the design. In October 2026, machine-assisted reviews of the payment code found further faults; all are fixed in version 0.1.15. There has been no independent audit. Treat it as unaudited software and use amounts you can afford to lose.

Not built. The win that pays itself, fair exchange on held tickets, and a seller on Core Lightning. What is planned is on Progress and status.

11Acknowledgements

Lightning Stream is a small addition to a great deal of other people's work. XBT is Bitcoin on BLAKE2b proof of work, and runs on Bitcoin Knots [60], maintained by Luke Dashjr, who wrote the BLAKE2b proof of work; the signature hash that keeps a payment from being replayed on the SHA-256 chain is Kyle Santiago's. Lightning on XBT exists because of Paul Lamb's Lightning Fork [61] and PrivKey's Core Lightning fork [63], built on lnd and Core Lightning. Paul also packages the node apps the app runs beside, and built the access grant that lets it connect on StartOS. Hashrate rental is built around OCEAN's DATUM [66].

Chris Guida's two reviews moved Lightning Stream onto Lightning and narrowed what it claims. Kyle Santiago pointed out the slot and dust limits the defaults are built around. Kilombino named the hashmaker and the hashtaker and runs a relay the app uses. The mistakes that remain are ours.
