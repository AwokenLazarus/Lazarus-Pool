Whitepaper · XBTLightning Stream: pay as it arrives

The people who run Lightning nodes should be able to connect any service they want to offer to the network (a file or media server, AI compute, storage, software of their own) and be paid for it piece by piece, by anyone. Sometimes that is one buyer and one seller, as with a streamed film. Sometimes it is many sellers at once, as in a torrent swarm.

Lightning Stream is an upgrade to Lightning built for that: a way for people and AI agents to pay for a service while it is being delivered, an answer token by token, a file chunk by chunk, an API call by call. A few small payments are locked ahead of the service, each request releases one, and the ones not used come back. For pieces too small to be worth a payment of their own, a fair roll decides which pieces are paid: the price is exact on average, and evens out only over many wins.

Draft 10 · 22 October 2026, updated 24 October 2026 · Lightning Stream (source). This draft is the short version: it explains the idea, and the detail lives on four pages of its own, listed below. Everything here is built and runs on a regtest, a private test network of our own nodes. On XBT mainnet it has run only in a rehearsal between two of our own nodes; see Status.

Lightning made payments fast. Stream tickets make them continuous.

1The problem

More and more of what we buy arrives over time: the tokens of a model's answer, the minutes of a stream, the pages of a dataset, the thousandth call to an API. Machines are now the buyers as often as people, and the prices are tiny: fractions of a cent, thousands of times an hour.

Most ways to pay for that ask someone to trust someone. Pay up front and you trust the seller to deliver. Pay afterwards and the seller trusts you. A subscription or an API key trusts a company with your card. An on-chain payment for each piece is honest but far too slow and too costly.

Lightning [3] already moves small payments between strangers in a moment, with nobody holding the money in the middle. What it lacks is a way to pay for something that arrives in thousands of pieces without making every piece wait for its payment. In our lab (Lightning Fork nodes on a regtest) one Lightning payment per piece took 110 ms on a direct channel and 210 ms through one routing node (median), and each payment through a routing node paid that node's base fee. Lightning Stream adds the missing part, and leaves the rest of Lightning as it is.

2The idea

Lock a few small Lightning payments before the service starts. Release one with each request. Take back the ones you did not use.

That is the whole addition. Lightning Stream is built on Lightning, not beside it: Lightning's own routing, liquidity and nodes carry everything else, and Lightning Stream runs no network and no hub of its own. It needs no change to an lnd-family node, that is lnd [62] and its forks, which have hold invoices; of that family only Lightning Fork [61], the fork of lnd that runs on XBT, has been run. A Core Lightning node can be a buyer's node or a routing node, but not a seller's node for held tickets today (which nodes can sell).

3How a ticket works

An ordinary Lightning payment is locked to a hash, and whoever shows the secret behind the hash collects it. Normally the seller picks that secret, so the money is the seller's the moment it arrives. A ticket turns that round: the buyer picks the secret.

Terms
The seller answers the first request with its price and terms: its Lightning node, the size of a ticket, how many tickets it will hold for one buyer, and how long a ticket lives.
Lock
The wallet makes one secret for each ticket and sends the seller only the hashes. For each hash the seller's node issues a hold invoice, which can be paid but not collected until its secret is shown. Hold invoices are not ours: Joost Jager added them to lnd in 2019 [5]. The wallet checks each invoice and pays it through the owner's Lightning node.
Pay
When the balance with the seller runs out, the wallet releases the next secret, in a short request of its own. The seller confirms that the matching ticket is locked, collects it, and serves requests from a ticket's worth of balance, counted in millisatoshis and signed for in a receipt.
Refill
The wallet keeps two tickets locked, and locks the next in the background as one is used. No request waits for a payment.
Stop
The seller cancels the unused tickets, and the buyer's money is free at once. If the seller does not, the tickets expire and the money returns by itself.

A payment that the payer releases in a second step is not our idea: Hiroki Gondo proposed "stuckless payments" for Lightning in 2019 [6], with point locks, which Lightning does not have yet. Tickets get a similar effect from hold invoices, which Lightning nodes already have. It has a price: the secret no longer proves payment, because the buyer always knew it. The proof is the seller's signed receipt, together with the settled payment on the buyer's own node.

What each side risks. The buyer can lose at most one ticket and its routing fee: a seller can take a released secret and serve nothing, but it cannot take the next one. Unused tickets are locked, not lost, until they are cancelled or expire; with 40-block tickets that took 25 to 51 blocks in our lab, about 3 to 6 hours at XBT's block spacing. A seller that serves only on a good secret for a locked ticket, and collects at once, has its payment committed to it; it should count a ticket as income only once its node no longer lists it as pending. Routing nodes carry the held tickets meanwhile. The full table of worst cases is under Design details.

Why the defaults are small. A ticket is 1,000 sat by default; 2 are held at a time with a seller the owner named and 1 with one it did not, for about 40 blocks. A held ticket takes a payment slot on every channel it crosses, and a channel with a Core Lightning node at either end has only 30 slots in each direction for everybody who uses it, so the wallet never holds more than half of them. And a held payment below a channel's dust limit cannot be claimed on the chain, so no held ticket is under 546 sat, or under a higher floor where the fee rate requires one: 2,304 sat through a routing node at mainnet's defaults. So on mainnet, at the defaults, no ticket is held through a routing node; those calls are paid by a prepaid ticket or by the lottery. Kyle Santiago pointed out both limits, slots and dust, and the defaults changed because of it. A seller the owner has not named is first paid one prepaid ticket, an ordinary payment it collects as it arrives, before anything is held with it. Slots and dust and Fee rates have the reasons and the figures.

4Smaller than a ticket: the lottery

Some things are worth less than the smallest payment worth sending. One piece of a shared file may be worth a tenth of a sat. Lightning carries nothing below 1 sat, a routing node charges about 1 sat to carry any payment at its default policy, and a held ticket is never under 546 sat. In a swarm of forty sellers, most will earn less than one ticket from a buyer.

The answer is old and simple, and it is not ours: David Wheeler and Ronald Rivest described it in 1996 and 1997 [33][34]. Do not pay for every piece. Roll a fair die for every piece instead. Most rolls pay nothing. Now and then a roll wins, and a win pays an amount large enough to be worth sending. The odds are set so that the average is the price. With pieces at a tenth of a sat and a win of 64 sat, the odds are 1 in 640: over 640 pieces the buyer pays 64 sat on average, which is a tenth of a sat for each. Most pieces move no money at all.

Neither side can steer the roll. Each roll is made from two halves. The seller fixes its half of every roll before the session and shows only a fingerprint of them, so it cannot change one later. The buyer adds its half only after the piece has arrived. The two are hashed together and the result decides, and both sides can work it out for themselves. The argument treats SHA-256 as a fair source of randomness, and it is our own. Chris Guida's second review read it and the code and found no way for either side to steer a roll; that was one person's reading, not a proof and not an audit.

Exact on average, uneven in one run. A miner knows the feeling: no block for a while, then one, and over a year the income matches the work. What one buyer pays one seller is a whole number of wins, so it evens out only over many wins between the same two parties: to be within 20% of the price nine times in ten takes about 68 wins (computed). Below that it is luck. So the wallet keeps a call that expects fewer than 4 wins off the lottery where the seller also offers prepaid tickets, and pays it with a ticket instead.

The form that is built: the buyer pays after a win. Nothing is locked ahead. When a roll wins, the seller sends an ordinary Lightning invoice, and the wallet, which has worked out the roll itself, pays it at once, and pays only a win it found. Its weakness is plain: a buyer can see a win and not pay. In our lab such a buyer was served about 1.4 wins' worth on average before the seller stopped it. That is a small, bounded loss to each cheat, not enforcement. Because nothing is held, neither slots nor dust stand in the way, which makes this form the way to pay many sellers at once.

The form that is not built: a win that pays itself. The buyer sets a ticket aside, and each roll gives the seller a small, hidden chance of drawing its key, so the buyer cannot refuse to pay a win. The aim was first set out and built by Paul Gerhart, Jay Taylor and Sri Aravinda Krishnan Thyagarajan in May 2026 [44]; our way of reaching it differs from theirs, rests on an argument of our own, and will not be built before a cryptographer has reviewed it. The lottery in detail has both forms.

5Many sellers, and how a buyer chooses

Nothing in a ticket ties a buyer to one seller: each ticket is its own Lightning payment. But the slots a held ticket takes are shared along its whole route. From one channel of 30 slots, keeping to half of them, a buyer can hold tickets with 7 sellers at two each, or 15 at one each. So held tickets are for one seller or a few, and a swarm is paid after a win in the lottery, which holds nothing. On a regtest, through one routing node on channels of 30 slots, the built wallet and gateway paid 100 sellers at once with no payment failed; the 100 sellers were 100 gateways beside one seller node.

The wallet chooses for each call, by the size of a piece and how long the buyer stays:

CaseHow it is paid
A piece worth a ticket or more, or a seller the buyer stays withTickets. With a seller the owner has not named, one prepaid ticket first, then held tickets one at a time.
Anything smaller, and any seller no ticket can be held with, as most sellers in a swarm areThe lottery, paid after a win.
A single call from a buyer with no sessionOne ordinary Lightning payment.

The full rule, with its edge cases, is under How it comes together.

6Paying over HTTP

A seller puts a gateway in front of any web service it already runs, beside its Lightning node. The gateway answers a first request with 402 Payment Required and its terms, in the shape the x402 standard gives that status [22], and every answer after that comes with a receipt the seller signs. The ticket and lottery entries are our own, so only Lightning Stream clients can pay with them.

The buyer can be anything that makes HTTP requests through a Lightning Stream client: a person's app or media player, a script, a server, or an AI agent. The client we have built is a wallet, made first for the hardest case, an AI agent, which should be able to spend but never to hold a key, because a model can be tricked by what it reads. So the agent asks the wallet to pay, over the Model Context Protocol [26], and the wallet decides: it holds the keys and the ticket secrets, keeps to a policy the owner signs (budgets, caps for each seller, and a small cap for sellers the owner has never heard of), and books what its node paid, never a number a seller sends. Mainnet is refused on both sides unless the owner asks for it in so many words. Uses has the wire, the wallet and what they are for.

7What it costs

Money tied up
Unused tickets are locked until the seller cancels them or they expire, for hours if the seller vanishes.
A Lightning node on each side
A seller needs an lnd-family node with inbound capacity, online while tickets are locked. A buyer needs a Lightning node with a channel.
Load on routing nodes
Held tickets take slots and capacity on every channel of their route, and a routing node is paid nothing for a ticket that is cancelled. On a large network that is the behaviour that defences against channel jamming [10] are meant to stop, which is why tickets stay few, small and short-lived.
A routing fee per ticket
At the forks' default policy a hop takes about 1 sat for each collected ticket: 0.1% of a 1,000 sat ticket. What nodes on mainnet charge is not known to us.
Real fee rates
Every on-chain figure was measured at regtest's 1 sat/vB, on channels of the anchor type. At mainnet's rates, on a channel without anchors, a 1,000 sat ticket can have no output on the chain at all, which is why the floor for held tickets rises with the fee rate.
An uneven lottery
Paid by lottery, income and cost are uneven in the short run, and the seller goes first by about one win's worth for each new buyer.
What others see
No transaction on the chain ties a buyer to a seller, but the nodes along a route see a run of equal payments to one seller.

The costs, stated plainly has the figures, and Fee rates the table at 1, 10 and 50 sat/vB.

8Prior art

Paying by lottery is an old idea, and none of it is ours. David Wheeler proposed "transactions using bets" in 1996 [33] and Ronald Rivest "electronic lottery tickets as micropayments" in 1997 [34], with a bank that honours the winning tickets; Silvio Micali and Rivest removed the back-and-forth in 2002 [35], and Rafael Pass and abhi shelat put an escrow on a chain in the bank's place in 2015 [36]. Orchid [39] and Livepeer [40] run such lotteries with a contract that pays a winner without the payer's help. For Bitcoin the idea was raised by Mike Caldwell in 2012 [41], sketched inside Lightning channels by Tadge Dryja in 2016 [42] and built from specially made keys by Oleksandr Kurbatov in 2025 [43], and a win over Lightning that enforces itself was first built by Gerhart, Taylor and Thyagarajan [44].

Where tickets sit. Between a prepaid balance and a payment per piece. A prepaid balance, such as L402 used for a metered service [23], hands the seller the money before the service, and the buyer can lose all of it. A payment per piece, such as the keysend streaming of podcast apps [8][9], hands it over piece by piece and makes each piece wait for its payment. A ticket commits the money before the service and hands it over a ticket at a time. Cashu ecash pays per request instantly and privately, but a mint holds the money [27]; a locked ticket is held by nobody. Nostr Wallet Connect gives each app a limited connection to a wallet [25], the idea the agent wallet applies with an owner-signed policy.

How Stream differs. Nothing in a ticket is a new mechanism: it is the hold invoice [5], used to the end "stuckless payments" were proposed for [6], and bound to HTTP 402 and to an agent's spending policy. The lottery as built adds nothing to the older work but that same binding. Lightning has neither a bank nor a contract to honour a win, which is why, in the form where the buyer pays after a win, the seller is exposed by about one win. Prior art, on the design page, has the full discussion.

9Status

Built, and run on a regtest only, between our own nodes: tickets on both sides, the ticket book in the wallet and the seller's gateway; the lottery in the form where the buyer pays after a win; a demo in which a film is paid for piece by piece as a player reads it; and packages for Umbrel and StartOS node boxes. On StartOS they cannot start beside Lightning Fork until a change to its package, which its maintainer has agreed to look at for the release after next. Lab scripts in the public tree rerun the tests, and a guide lets anyone outside the project run them.

On mainnet, a rehearsal between our own nodes. On 23 and 24 October 2026 a buyer and a seller of our own, on one machine, paid each other on XBT mainnet over one direct channel of 100,000 sat: a page and a 5 MB file with tickets, and 20 small pieces by lottery. Both runs paid what they should, and the bodies matched their files. The first run found four faults, which were fixed before the second; On mainnet has the figures. Nothing has yet run through anyone else's node or with anyone outside the project. The next step is a small test with people outside the project, by invitation, with amounts that can be lost entirely. It has not started.

Not audited. Chris Guida has reviewed the code and the design twice, and his findings are answered in the code and on these pages; nobody outside the project has reviewed those answers. There has been no independent audit, and the win that pays itself waits for a cryptographer. Treat it all as unaudited software.

11Acknowledgements

Lightning Stream is a small addition to a great deal of other people's work, and the credit for that work is theirs.

XBT is Bitcoin on BLAKE2b proof of work, and it runs on Bitcoin Knots [60], maintained by Luke Dashjr, who wrote the BLAKE2b proof of work; the unified signature hash that keeps a payment from being replayed on the SHA-256 chain is Kyle Santiago's. Lightning on XBT exists because of Paul Lamb's Lightning Fork [61] and the Core Lightning fork by PrivKey [63], which stand on lnd by Lightning Labs [62] and on Core Lightning. Every measurement in this paper was made in the regtest lab that Lightning Fork's author publishes: most on Lightning Fork nodes alone, and the slot and dust measurements with the released binaries of the Core Lightning fork beside them. The light client of the removed channel talked to electrs by Roman Zeyde [64], all signing runs in libsecp256k1 [65], and pay with work is built around OCEAN's DATUM and TIDES [66]. The demo's media server is Jellyfin, as its project ships it, and its film is Big Buck Bunny, (c) 2008 Blender Foundation, www.bigbuckbunny.org, under Creative Commons Attribution 3.0. The source tree's THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES.md lists every library it links, with its licence.

The ideas are older than the software. One-way channels are Jeremy Spilman's, Lightning is Joseph Poon's and Tadge Dryja's, hold invoices are Joost Jager's, and a payment the payer releases in a second step is Hiroki Gondo's. Paying by lottery is David Wheeler's and Ronald Rivest's, and a win that enforces itself over Lightning is Paul Gerhart's, Jay Taylor's and Sri Aravinda Krishnan Thyagarajan's. The HTTP shape is x402's, and the agent's tools are spoken in the Model Context Protocol.

Chris Guida read the earlier design and its code in October 2026 and found what we had missed. Moving onto Lightning follows from his review. He then read the ticket design, the lottery and the tree a second time, and most of what was new in Draft 9 follows from that second review: the narrower claim about which nodes need no change, the costs at real fee rates and the floor that follows, the prepaid first ticket for strangers, the bounds on the lottery and its horizon, the guard against mainnet by omission, and evidence that can be checked from outside. The credit for both is his. The mistakes that remain are ours.

Kyle Santiago read the design and pointed out both limits that the defaults are built around: the 30 pending payments a Core Lightning channel carries, and the dust limits. The defaults changed because of that reading.
